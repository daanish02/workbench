Almost every statistical claim rests on a sample. Whether the sample can be trusted depends on how it was drawn and how large it is. Random selection removes systematic error and leaves only sampling noise, which shrinks with size. Bias, by contrast, does not shrink with more data. This note covers the standard error of a proportion, how to choose a sample size, and a simulation that shows a large biased sample being confidently wrong. The [Law of Large Numbers](../../probability/law-of-large-numbers/) is why random-sampling noise shrinks as the sample grows.

## Random samples and the margin of error

For a proportion $p$ estimated from $n$ random respondents, the standard error is $\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$. The $95\%$ margin of error is about $1.96$ times this. The product $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = \frac{1}{2}$, so the worst case is $\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$.

```python
import math

for n in [100, 400, 1000, 1068]:
    moe = 1.96 * math.sqrt(0.25 / n)
    print(f"n = {n:>5}: worst-case 95% margin of error = {moe * 100:.2f} points")
# n =   100: worst-case 95% margin of error = 9.80 points
# n =   400: worst-case 95% margin of error = 4.90 points
# n =  1000: worst-case 95% margin of error = 3.10 points
# n =  1068: worst-case 95% margin of error = 3.00 points
```

In words: a sample of $100$ gives a margin of about $\pm 9.8$ points, and $400$ gives about $\pm 4.9$. Quadrupling the sample halves the margin.

## Choosing a sample size

Solving for $n$ gives the sample size needed for a target margin $E$: $n = \left(\frac{1.96}{E}\right)^2 p(1-p)$. For $E = 0.03$ and $p = \frac{1}{2}$ this is about $1067.1$, rounded up to $1068$.

```python
import math

E, p = 0.03, 0.5
n_needed = math.ceil((1.96 / E) ** 2 * p * (1 - p))
print("sample size for +/- 3 points at 95%:", n_needed)
# sample size for +/- 3 points at 95%: 1068
```

In words: about a thousand respondents give a margin of three points, which is why national polls often report that size. Going from three points to one point would need about nine times as many people.

## Bias does not average out

Suppose a population of $10000$ people, $40\%$ of whom support a policy. A survey that reaches only the people who answer their phones, where support is $60\%$, will be wrong no matter how large it is. The simulation below compares a random sample of $1000$ with a biased sample of $1000$ drawn from the reachable group, using a fixed seed.

```python
import random

random.seed(8)
# population: 4000 supporters, 6000 opponents, but supporters are more reachable
pop = [1] * 4000 + [0] * 6000
random_sample = random.sample(pop, 1000)
reachable = [1] * 2400 + [0] * 1600  # reachable group: 60% supporters
biased_sample = random.sample(reachable, 1000)
print("true support: 0.400")
print("random sample estimate:", round(sum(random_sample) / 1000, 3), " biased sample estimate:", round(sum(biased_sample) / 1000, 3))
# true support: 0.400
# random sample estimate: 0.395  biased sample estimate: 0.616
```

In words: the random sample lands near the true $40\%$. The biased sample lands near $62\%$ and is just as precise. Its margin of error looks small, but it estimates the wrong group. Only better selection fixes this, not a larger sample.

<Callout type="pitfall">
A narrow margin of error only measures random noise. It says nothing about whether the sample represents the population. A huge convenience sample can have a tiny margin of error and still be badly biased.
</Callout>

## Designing a sample

Define the population first, then choose a sampling frame that covers it, draw randomly from that frame, and check for non-response. Only after that does the sample size and margin of error matter. The order is what keeps the estimate honest.
