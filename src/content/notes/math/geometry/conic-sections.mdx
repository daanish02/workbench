The ancient Greeks cut a cone with a plane and noticed that the edge of the cut is one of four curves. Apollonius wrote about them around 200 BCE, and some eighteen centuries later Kepler found that planets move on ellipses. The same curves appear wherever something is focused: a parabolic mirror brings parallel light to a point, and a planet's orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. This note defines the four conics, gives their equations, and checks the defining distance properties numerically.

## The cone and the plane

Take a double cone and a plane. If the plane is perpendicular to the axis, the cut is a circle. Tilt it slightly and the cut is an ellipse. Tilt it until it is parallel to one side of the cone and the cut is a parabola. Tilt it more and the plane cuts both nappes of the cone, giving a hyperbola with two separate branches.

The angle of the plane decides the type, and the eccentricity $e$ makes that angle a number. It is the ratio of the distance from any point on the curve to a focus, over its distance to a fixed line called the directrix.

## Focus and directrix

Pick a point $F$ (the focus), a line $D$ (the directrix) and a number $e$. The set of points $P$ with $|PF| = e \cdot \mathrm{dist}(P, D)$ is a conic. The eccentricity sorts them:

| $e$ | Curve |
| --- | --- |
| $0$ | circle |
| $0 < e < 1$ | ellipse |
| $e = 1$ | parabola |
| $e > 1$ | hyperbola |

The circle is a limit case: a circle is the set of points at a fixed distance from its centre, and its eccentricity is zero because the focus and centre coincide.

## Ellipse

An ellipse has two foci. Its standard equation is

$$
\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1, \qquad a > b > 0,

$$

where $a$ is the semi-major axis and $b$ the semi-minor axis. The foci sit at $(\pm c, 0)$ with $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$, and $e = c/a$. The defining property is that the sum of distances to the two foci is constant, equal to $2a$.

The script below takes $a = 5$ and $b = 3$, so $c = 4$ and $e = 0.8$. It then samples 360 points on the ellipse and checks that the distances to the two foci always sum to $2a = 10$.

```python
import math

a, b = 5.0, 3.0
c = math.sqrt(a * a - b * b)
e = c / a
print("foci at x = ±", c, " eccentricity e =", e)

F1, F2 = (-c, 0.0), (c, 0.0)
def point_on_ellipse(t):
    return (a * math.cos(t), b * math.sin(t))

worst = max(abs(math.dist(point_on_ellipse(t), F1) + math.dist(point_on_ellipse(t), F2) - 2 * a)
            for t in [i * 2 * math.pi / 360 for i in range(360)])
print("max |PF1 + PF2 - 2a| over 360 points:", f"{worst:.2e}")
# foci at x = ± 4.0  eccentricity e = 0.8
# max |PF1 + PF2 - 2a| over 360 points: 1.78e-15
```

In words: the foci are at $\pm 4$, the eccentricity is $0.8$, and the largest deviation of $PF_1 + PF_2$ from $10$ is about $10^{-15}$. That is floating-point rounding, so the constant-sum property holds on the curve.

<Callout type="pitfall">
The focal distance has a different sign in each conic: $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ for the ellipse but $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ for the hyperbola. For the ellipse, $a$ must also be the larger semi-axis: in $\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 1$ the major axis is vertical, so $a = 5$, $b = 3$ and the foci are at $(0, \pm 4)$, not on the $x$-axis.
</Callout>

## Parabola

A parabola has one focus and one directrix. In the standard form $y^2 = 4px$, the focus is $(p, 0)$ and the directrix is $x = -p$. The vertex is at the origin, and the curve opens toward the focus.

The shape of a satellite dish or a headlight reflector follows from this. A ray parallel to the axis hits the curve and reflects through the focus, because the focus is the point where all such reflections meet.

## Hyperbola

A hyperbola has two foci and two branches. Its standard form is $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$, with foci at $(\pm c, 0)$ where $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, and $e = c/a > 1$. The defining property is the opposite of the ellipse's: the difference of distances to the two foci is constant, $|PF_1 - PF_2| = 2a$.

The check below samples points on both branches of a hyperbola with $a = 5$ and $b = 3$, and confirms the difference of distances is $2a = 10$.

```python
import math

a, b = 5.0, 3.0
c_h = math.sqrt(a * a + b * b)
F1h, F2h = (-c_h, 0.0), (c_h, 0.0)
def point_on_hyperbola(t):
    return (a * math.cosh(t), b * math.sinh(t))

worst_h = max(abs(abs(math.dist(point_on_hyperbola(t), F1h) - math.dist(point_on_hyperbola(t), F2h)) - 2 * a)
              for t in [i / 100 for i in range(-300, 301)])
print("hyperbola error |PF1 - PF2| - 2a:", f"{worst_h:.2e}")
# hyperbola error |PF1 - PF2| - 2a: 7.11e-15
```

In words: the error is about $7 \times 10^{-15}$, so every sampled point has the constant difference. The right branch is the points closer to the right focus, and the left branch is the mirror image.

The parabola's check is the same idea with one focus. For $y^2 = 4px$ with $p = 1$, the focus is $(1, 0)$ and the directrix is $x = -1$, and every point is equally far from both.

```python
import math

p = 1.0
worst_p = max(abs(math.dist((t * t / (4 * p), t), (p, 0.0)) - (t * t / (4 * p) + p))
              for t in [i / 10 for i in range(-50, 51)])  # points (y^2/4p, y)
print("parabola error |PF - dist(P, directrix)|:", f"{worst_p:.1e}")
# parabola error |PF - dist(P, directrix)|: 4.4e-16
```

In words: the distance to the focus equals the distance to the directrix at every sampled point, up to rounding, which is the $e = 1$ case of the focus–directrix definition.

## The general quadratic equation

Every conic is a degree-two equation $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$, and its type can be read without completing squares. The quadratic part $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2$ is a [quadratic form](../../linear-algebra/quadratic-forms/) with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} A & B/2 \ B/2 & C \end{pmatrix}$, so the sign of its determinant, or equivalently of the **discriminant** $B^2 - 4AC$, decides the shape:

| $B^2 - 4AC$ | Eigenvalues of the form | Curve (non-degenerate case) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $< 0$ | same sign | ellipse (a circle when $A = C$ and $B = 0$) |
| $= 0$ | one zero | parabola |
| $> 0$ | opposite signs | hyperbola |

The $Bxy$ term only rotates the curve; the eigenvectors of the matrix are its axes.

```python
import numpy as np

def classify(A, B, C):
    disc = B * B - 4 * A * C
    kind = "ellipse" if disc < 0 else "parabola" if disc == 0 else "hyperbola"
    axes = np.linalg.eigh(np.array([[A, B / 2], [B / 2, C]]))[1]
    tilt = np.degrees(np.arctan2(axes[1, 0], axes[0, 0])) % 180
    return disc, kind, tilt

for coeffs in [(1, 0, 4), (1, 2, 1), (1, 3, 1), (5, 4, 2)]:
    disc, kind, tilt = classify(*coeffs)
    print(f"A, B, C = {coeffs}: B^2 - 4AC = {disc:>3} -> {kind}, axis at {tilt:.1f} deg")
# A, B, C = (1, 0, 4): B^2 - 4AC = -16 -> ellipse, axis at 0.0 deg
# A, B, C = (1, 2, 1): B^2 - 4AC =   0 -> parabola, axis at 135.0 deg
# A, B, C = (1, 3, 1): B^2 - 4AC =   5 -> hyperbola, axis at 135.0 deg
# A, B, C = (5, 4, 2): B^2 - 4AC = -24 -> ellipse, axis at 116.6 deg
```

In words: $x^2 + 4y^2$ is an ellipse with no tilt, $x^2 + 2xy + y^2 = (x + y)^2$ gives a parabola, $x^2 + 3xy + y^2$ a hyperbola, and $5x^2 + 4xy + 2y^2$ a tilted ellipse whose axes are the eigenvectors. Degenerate cases, such as a pair of lines or a single point, have the same discriminant as their family, so the test describes the shape when the equation has a curve at all.

## Where conics appear

Planetary orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus (Kepler's first law). Telescope and radio dishes are paraboloids, so incoming parallel signals gather at the focus. A cone of light from a lamp cut by a wall gives a conic. The properties above are why these shapes are used on purpose.
