Geometry is the oldest branch of mathematics that still looks the way it did two thousand years ago. Euclid's *Elements* gathered what Greek mathematicians knew into a chain of definitions, postulates and proofs, and that structure is what we still mean by a proof today. This note covers the basic objects, the rules for when two figures are the same or nearly the same, and the theorems that connect angles to lengths. Later notes in this section put these objects on coordinates and move them with transformations.

## Basic objects

A **point** has position and no size. A **line** is straight and extends forever in both directions. A **segment** is the part of a line between two points. An **angle** is formed by two rays from a common point, and it is measured in degrees ($360^\circ$ for a full turn) or radians.

Two lines are **parallel** when they never meet. Euclid's fifth postulate says that through a point off a line, exactly one line runs parallel to it. Much of the geometry in this note depends on that postulate, and the sphere is the standard example of a surface where it fails.

## Angles in a triangle

Draw a triangle and extend one side. The exterior angle equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles, and the three interior angles together make a straight line of $180^\circ$. The proof uses a parallel line through one vertex, which is why the result needs the parallel postulate.

The script below samples 1,000 random triangles and checks the angle sum. The angles are computed with the dot-product formula $\cos\theta = \frac{u \cdot v}{|u||v|}$, so the check is numerical, not a proof.

```python
import math
import random

random.seed(1)

def angles(p, q, r):
    def ang(a, b, c):  # the angle at vertex b
        v1 = (a[0] - b[0], a[1] - b[1])
        v2 = (c[0] - b[0], c[1] - b[1])
        cosv = (v1[0] * v2[0] + v1[1] * v2[1]) / (math.hypot(*v1) * math.hypot(*v2))
        return math.degrees(math.acos(max(-1, min(1, cosv))))
    return ang(q, p, r), ang(p, q, r), ang(p, r, q)

worst = 0.0
for _ in range(1000):
    pts = [(random.uniform(-10, 10), random.uniform(-10, 10)) for _ in range(3)]
    worst = max(worst, abs(sum(angles(*pts)) - 180))
print("max |sum of angles - 180| over 1000 triangles:", f"{worst:.2e}")
# max |sum of angles - 180| over 1000 triangles: 2.51e-11
```

In words: the largest deviation from $180^\circ$ over the 1,000 triangles is about $2.5 \times 10^{-11}$, which is rounding error. Every triangle sums to $180^\circ$ in the flat plane, and the check confirms the theorem for this sample rather than proving it.

## Congruence

Two figures are **congruent** when one can be moved onto the other, with the same sides and angles. For triangles you do not need all six measurements; three are enough, in one of three patterns:

- **SSS:** all three sides match.
- **SAS:** two sides and the angle between them match.
- **ASA:** two angles and the side between them match.

SAS is the rule that really fixes the triangle. Once two sides and the angle between them are known, the third side is determined, as the law of cosines shows: $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\gamma$.

```python
import math

s1, s2, theta = 3.0, 5.0, math.radians(60)
third = math.sqrt(s1 * s1 + s2 * s2 - 2 * s1 * s2 * math.cos(theta))
print("law of cosines third side (60 degrees between 3 and 5):", round(third, 4))
# law of cosines third side (60 degrees between 3 and 5): 4.3589
```

In words: two sides of lengths 3 and 5 with a $60^\circ$ angle between them force the third side to be about $4.36$. Any triangle with those three measurements is congruent to this one, which is the SAS rule at work.

<Callout type="pitfall">
SSA does not guarantee congruence. Two sides and an angle that is not between them can produce two different triangles. The angle must sit between the two known sides.
</Callout>

## Pythagoras and right triangles

In a right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. The $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle is the classic whole-number case: $9 + 16 = 25$. The theorem is the special case of the law of cosines with $\gamma = 90^\circ$, where $\cos\gamma = 0$.

## Similarity

Two figures are **similar** when they have the same angles and their lengths differ by a common factor $k$. Under a scale factor $k$, every length multiplies by $k$ and every area by $k^2$. This is why doubling a map's scale quadruples the area of a country on it.

```python
def area(p, q, r):
    return abs((q[0] - p[0]) * (r[1] - p[1]) - (r[0] - p[0]) * (q[1] - p[1])) / 2

tri = [(0, 0), (4, 0), (1, 3)]
k = 2.5
big = [(k * x, k * y) for x, y in tri]
print("area ratio for scale 2.5:", area(*big) / area(*tri), "expected", k * k)
# area ratio for scale 2.5: 6.25 expected 6.25
```

In words: the area of the scaled triangle is $6.25$ times the original, which is $2.5^2$. The area formula used here is the shoelace formula, which works from coordinates and is the bridge to the [Coordinate Geometry](../coordinate-geometry/) note.

## Why the parallel postulate matters

On a sphere, lines are great circles and every two of them meet. A triangle drawn on a sphere with three right angles has angles summing to $270^\circ$. The Euclidean theorems here hold for the flat plane, and the sphere shows what changes when the postulate is dropped. The [Differential Geometry](../differential-geometry/) note returns to this with geodesics.
