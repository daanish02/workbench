Bayes' rule answers a question people ask constantly in reverse: given that a test is positive, how likely is the condition? The direct information is usually the other way round, how likely a positive test is when the condition is present. This note builds the rule from conditional probability, works two examples with checked numbers, and shows why the base rate has to be part of the answer.

## Conditional probability

The probability of $A$ given $B$ is

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) > 0.
$$

Think of it as restricting attention to the outcomes where $B$ holds and asking what fraction of those also have $A$. Consider a die and let $B$ be "the roll is even" and $A$ be "the roll is a $4$". Then $P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{6}$ and $P(B) = \frac{1}{2}$, so $P(A \mid B) = \frac{1}{3}$.

```python
from fractions import Fraction

P_A_and_B = Fraction(1, 6)
P_B = Fraction(3, 6)
print("P(4 | even) =", P_A_and_B / P_B, "=", round(float(P_A_and_B / P_B), 4))
# P(4 | even) = 1/3 = 0.3333
```

In words: once you know the roll is even, there are three equally likely faces, and one of them is $4$.

## Bayes' rule

The definition can be rearranged two ways: $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)$ and also $P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$. Setting the two equal and dividing by $P(B)$ gives the rule:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}.
$$

The denominator is the total probability of the evidence. Split it by whether $A$ holds:

$$
P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^c)\,P(A^c).
$$

## Medical testing

A disease affects $1\%$ of a population. A test detects it with probability $0.90$ when present (sensitivity), and gives a false positive with probability $0.05$ when absent. A person tests positive. What is the chance they have the disease?

```python
prev, sens, fpr = 0.01, 0.90, 0.05
p_pos = sens * prev + fpr * (1 - prev)
posterior = sens * prev / p_pos
print("P(positive) =", round(p_pos, 4), " P(disease | positive) =", round(posterior, 4))
# P(positive) = 0.0585  P(disease | positive) = 0.1538
```

In words: a positive result is rare overall, $5.85\%$ of tests, and only about $15\%$ of those positives come from sick people.

The check below does the same count with a population of ten thousand people, which is often easier to trust than the formula.

```python
people = 10000
sick = people * 0.01
healthy = people - sick
true_pos = sick * 0.90
false_pos = healthy * 0.05
print("sick:", int(sick), " positive tests:", int(true_pos + false_pos), " sick among positives:", int(true_pos))  # prints: sick: 100  positive tests: 585  sick among positives: 90
print("fraction of positives who are sick:", round(true_pos / (true_pos + false_pos), 4))  # prints: fraction of positives who are sick: 0.1538
```

In words: of $585$ positive tests, only $90$ belong to sick people. A positive result is strong evidence compared with the $1\%$ base rate, yet most positives are false because the disease is rare. The rule and the counting give the same answer, $0.1538$.

<Callout type="pitfall">
Do not read $P(\text{positive} \mid \text{disease}) = 0.90$ as $P(\text{disease} \mid \text{positive})$. These are different probabilities. Swapping them is the base-rate fallacy, and it is the most common error in reading test results.
</Callout>

## Spam filtering

A mail filter learns from past mail. Suppose $20\%$ of mail is spam, the word "free" appears in $50\%$ of spam and in $2\%$ of legitimate mail. Given that a message contains "free", what is the probability it is spam?

```python
p_spam, p_free_spam, p_free_ham = 0.20, 0.50, 0.02
p_free = p_free_spam * p_spam + p_free_ham * (1 - p_spam)
print("P(spam | free) =", round(p_free_spam * p_spam / p_free, 4))
# P(spam | free) = 0.8621
```

In words: the word is rare in legitimate mail, so its presence shifts the odds sharply toward spam, from $20\%$ to about $86\%$. Real filters combine many such words, which needs the joint and conditional distributions in the next note.

## Updating

Bayes' rule is an update rule. The posterior from one piece of evidence becomes the prior for the next. In **odds form** the update is a single multiplication:

$$
\frac{P(A \mid B)}{P(A^c \mid B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B \mid A^c)} \cdot \frac{P(A)}{P(A^c)},

$$

posterior odds equal the **likelihood ratio** times the prior odds. For the medical test the ratio is $0.90 / 0.05 = 18$, so prior odds of $1:99$ become $18:99$, which is the $0.1538$ found above. When several pieces of evidence are independent given the cause, as a naive Bayes spam filter assumes for words, their likelihood ratios simply multiply.

```python
prior_odds = 0.01 / 0.99
lr = 0.90 / 0.05
post_odds = lr * prior_odds
print("posterior after one positive:", round(post_odds / (1 + post_odds), 4))  # prints: posterior after one positive: 0.1538
post_odds *= lr  # a second, independent positive test
print("posterior after two positives:", round(post_odds / (1 + post_odds), 4))  # prints: posterior after two positives: 0.766
```

In words: one positive test moves the chance from $1\%$ to about $15\%$; a second independent positive multiplies the odds by $18$ again and moves it to about $77\%$.
