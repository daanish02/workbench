Add up many small independent effects and the total looks like a bell curve, whatever each effect looked like. Heights, measurement errors, the total of many small transactions, and the average of many dice all share this shape. The central limit theorem explains why, and it is the reason the normal distribution appears in statistics so often. This note states the theorem, checks it against the exact binomial, and then shows the convergence with a simulation that starts from a flat distribution.

## The statement

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent with the same mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Then the standardised sum

$$
Z_n = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}
$$

has a distribution that approaches the standard normal as $n \to \infty$. Equivalently, the sample mean $\bar X_n$ is approximately normal with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.

## An exact check with coin flips

A sum of $n$ fair coin flips is binomial. For $n = 50$ the exact probability that the sum is at most $25$ is computable, and the normal approximation with a continuity correction should match it closely.

```python
import math

def Phi(x):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))

n, p = 50, 0.5
exact = sum(math.comb(n, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (n - k) for k in range(0, 26))
mu, sd = n * p, math.sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))
approx = Phi((25.5 - mu) / sd)
print("exact P(X <= 25):", round(exact, 6), " normal approximation:", round(approx, 6))  # prints: exact P(X <= 25): 0.556138 normal approximation: 0.556231
```

In words: the exact value $0.556138$ and the approximation $0.556231$ agree to three decimal places, even though the sum is discrete and the normal is continuous. The continuity correction adds $\frac{1}{2}$ because $X \le 25$ on integers covers the continuous range up to $25.5$.

## A simulation from a flat start

The uniform distribution on $[0, 1]$ has mean $\frac{1}{2}$ and variance $\frac{1}{12}$, and its density is flat, not bell-shaped. The script below averages $30$ uniform values, repeats that $20000$ times with a fixed seed, and compares the spread of the averages with the prediction $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.

```python
import math
import random

random.seed(11)
n, reps = 30, 20000
means = [sum(random.random() for _ in range(n)) / n for _ in range(reps)]
m = sum(means) / reps
sd = math.sqrt(sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in means) / reps)
predicted = math.sqrt(1 / 12) / math.sqrt(n)
print("mean of averages:", round(m, 4), " sd of averages:", round(sd, 4), " predicted sd:", round(predicted, 4))  # prints: mean of averages: 0.4997 sd of averages: 0.0533 predicted sd: 0.0527
```

In words: the averages centre on $0.5$, and their spread, $0.0533$, is close to the predicted $\sigma / \sqrt{n} \approx 0.0527$. The check does not show the shape. A histogram would show a bell curve even though each uniform value is flat.

## How much the shape matters

The shape of the average is normal, but only roughly, and the approximation is slowest in the tails. A useful check counts how many simulated means fall within two standard errors of $\frac{1}{2}$. The CLT predicts about $95\%$.

```python
import math
import random

random.seed(11)
n, reps = 30, 20000
means = [sum(random.random() for _ in range(n)) / n for _ in range(reps)]
se = math.sqrt(1 / 12) / math.sqrt(n)
share = sum(1 for x in means if abs(x - 0.5) <= 1.96 * se) / reps
print("share of means within 1.96 standard errors:", round(share, 3))  # prints: share of means within 1.96 standard errors: 0.95
```

In words: $0.95$ matches the predicted $95\%$ for a sample of thirty. For a skewed starting distribution, such as the exponential, the approximation needs more terms to reach the same accuracy, so the convergence is real but its speed depends on the shape. The **Berry–Esseen theorem** makes this precise: the largest gap between the distribution of the standardised mean and the normal is at most a constant times $\frac{E|X - \mu|^3}{\sigma^3\sqrt{n}}$, so it shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$ and grows with the skewness of the source.

<Callout type="pitfall">
The CLT needs finite variance and independent terms. It does not say that every large dataset is normal, and it does not apply to the raw data. It applies to averages or sums of independent values. Heavy-tailed data, such as incomes or some financial returns, can have infinite variance, and then the normal approximation fails.
</Callout>

## Why it matters

Confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and most of classical statistics rest on the CLT. It is why you can say how far a sample mean is likely to be from the true mean without knowing the underlying distribution, provided the sample is large enough and the variance is finite.
