Probability has to say what an event is before it can give an event a probability. In a finite experiment every subset can be assigned a probability, and the earlier notes did that without thinking. With continuous outcomes such as a point on a line, the set of all subsets is too large to handle consistently. Measure theory fixes the collection of allowed events and the rules for measuring them. This note states the framework, checks the axioms on a finite example where every step can be computed, and explains why the framework is needed.

## Sigma-algebras: the events

Let $\Omega$ be the sample space. A **sigma-algebra** $\mathcal F$ is a collection of subsets of $\Omega$ such that

1. $\Omega \in \mathcal F$,
2. if $A \in \mathcal F$ then its complement $A^c \in \mathcal F$,
3. if $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal F$ then their union $\bigcup_i A_i \in \mathcal F$.

These rules say the events can be combined by complement and by countable union, and the results are still events. For a finite $\Omega$ the countable union is an ordinary union.

The script below takes $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and builds the smallest sigma-algebra that contains the event $\{1, 2\}$, by closing under complement and union until nothing new appears.

```python
omega = frozenset({1, 2, 3, 4})
F = {frozenset(), omega, frozenset({1, 2})}
changed = True
while changed:
    changed = False
    new = {omega - A for A in F} | {A | B for A in F for B in F}
    if not new <= F:
        F |= new
        changed = True
print("number of events:", len(F))  # prints: number of events: 4
print("events:", sorted((sorted(A) for A in F), key=lambda s: (len(s), s)))  # prints: events: [[], [1, 2], [3, 4], [1, 2, 3, 4]]
```

In words: the four events are the empty set, the two halves $\{1, 2\}$ and $\{3, 4\}$, and the whole space. These four are closed under complement and union. The event $\{1\}$ is not among them, so it cannot be given a probability in this model.

## Probability measures

A **probability measure** on $(\Omega, \mathcal F)$ is a function $P$ on events with $P(A) \ge 0$, $P(\Omega) = 1$, and additivity over disjoint events. For finite spaces additivity is enough, and the measure of any event is the sum of the probabilities of its outcomes.

```python
omega = frozenset({1, 2, 3, 4})
weights = {1: 0.1, 2: 0.4, 3: 0.2, 4: 0.3}
P = lambda A: sum(weights[w] for w in A)
print("P(omega) =", round(P(omega), 6), " P({1,2}) =", round(P({1, 2}), 6), " P({3,4}) =", round(P({3, 4}), 6))  # prints: P(omega) = 1.0  P({1,2}) = 0.5  P({3,4}) = 0.5
```

In words: the measure of the whole space is $1$, and the two disjoint halves add to $1$. A measure on the four-element space is determined by its values on single outcomes.

## Why the axioms are needed

On the real line the natural measure is length, the Lebesgue measure, which gives the interval $[a, b]$ the measure $b - a$. It is possible to build sets that cannot be given a length consistent with additivity, such as the Vitali set, which is constructed with the axiom of choice. So the family of events must be restricted to sets that can be measured, and the Borel sets, generated by the open intervals, are the usual choice.

No finite computation shows this. What a check can show is that the rules hold on a finite example, which is why the example above closes the family explicitly.

<Callout type="pitfall">
Do not assume that every subset of the real line has a probability. The collection of all subsets is too large to be assigned lengths consistently. When a textbook says 'the event $A$' for a continuous variable, it means a measurable set, and the sets that arise in practice, such as intervals and their countable unions, are all measurable.
</Callout>

## Why probability needs it

Limits of events, conditional expectations and the convergence theorems used in the law of large numbers and in stochastic calculus all require countable unions and limits. The sigma-algebra is what makes those operations stay inside the set of events. Most applied work uses the intuition from the earlier notes, and this framework is the reason that intuition is trustworthy.
