A single random variable describes one uncertain number. A stochastic process describes an uncertain quantity that changes over time, such as the price of a stock, the position of a particle, or the number of customers who have arrived by noon. This note introduces the idea through two examples, the simple random walk and the Poisson process, and checks their basic properties both exactly and by simulation.

## What a process is

A stochastic process is a collection of random variables $\{X_t\}$ indexed by time $t$. Time can be discrete ($t = 0, 1, 2, \dots$) or continuous. Fixing one outcome of all the randomness gives a **path**, a function of time. The process is the rule that generates these paths and assigns them probabilities.

## The simple random walk

Start at $S_0 = 0$ and at each step add $+1$ or $-1$ with equal probability. After $n$ steps, $S_n$ is a sum of $n$ independent $\pm 1$ values. Each step has mean $0$ and variance $1$, so $S_n$ has mean $0$ and variance $n$.

The script below enumerates all $2^{10} = 1024$ paths of length $10$ and checks these two facts, along with $P(S_{10} = 0)$.

```python
from itertools import product

n = 10
ends = [sum(steps) for steps in product((-1, 1), repeat=n)]
mean = sum(ends) / len(ends)
var = sum((e - mean) ** 2 for e in ends) / len(ends)
p_zero = sum(1 for e in ends if e == 0) / len(ends)
print("mean of S_10:", mean, " variance of S_10:", var)  # prints: mean of S_10: 0.0  variance of S_10: 10.0
print("P(S_10 = 0):", round(p_zero, 4), " = C(10,5)/1024 =", round(252 / 1024, 4))  # prints: P(S_10 = 0): 0.2461  = C(10,5)/1024 = 0.2461
```

In words: the variance grows linearly in time, so the typical distance from the start grows like $\sqrt{n}$. That square-root growth is the signature of a random walk: the typical distance $\sqrt{n}$ grows more slowly than any steady drift proportional to $n$, yet the walk never settles down.

## The Poisson process

The Poisson process counts arrivals at rate $\lambda$ per unit time. Two facts describe it. The number of arrivals in an interval of length $T$ is Poisson with mean $\lambda T$, and the gaps between consecutive arrivals are independent exponential variables with mean $\frac{1}{\lambda}$.

The script below simulates gaps with a fixed seed and counts arrivals in an interval of length $10$ at rate $2$, where the mean count should be $20$.

```python
import random

random.seed(5)
lam, T, runs = 2.0, 10.0, 5000
counts = []
for _ in range(runs):
    t, k = 0.0, 0
    while True:
        t += random.expovariate(lam)
        if t > T:
            break
        k += 1
    counts.append(k)
mean_count = sum(counts) / runs
print("mean arrivals in [0, 10]:", round(mean_count, 2), " predicted lambda T = 20")  # prints: mean arrivals in [0, 10]: 20.02 predicted lambda T = 20
```

In words: the simulated mean of $20.02$ is close to the predicted $20$. The simulation only draws exponential gaps and adds them up, and the Poisson count comes out on its own, which is the link between the two descriptions of the process.

<Callout type="pitfall">
A process is more than its marginal distributions. Two processes can have the same distribution at each fixed time and still have very different paths. The random walk and a process that jumps to a fresh random value each step can have similar marginal spreads but look nothing alike when you plot them.
</Callout>
