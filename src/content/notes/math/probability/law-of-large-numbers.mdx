Flip a fair coin a hundred times and the proportion of heads is usually near one half. Flip it a million times and the proportion is even closer. That steady settling is the law of large numbers, and it is the reason averages are a reliable way to learn an expected value from data. This note states the law, shows it with a seeded simulation, and separates what the law says from a common misreading.

## The statement

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent and identically distributed with mean $\mu$, and let $\bar X_n$ be the average of the first $n$ values. The law says $\bar X_n$ approaches $\mu$ as $n$ grows. The weak form is a statement about probabilities: for any tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$,

$$
P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| > \varepsilon\big) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.
$$

The strong form says that, with probability $1$, the sequence of averages actually converges to $\mu$. The strong form is the stronger claim, and both need only a finite mean.

## A simulation with a fair die

The script below rolls a die with a fixed seed so the output is repeatable. It prints the running average at several sample sizes. The expected value is $3.5$.

```python
import random

random.seed(2024)
rolls = [random.randint(1, 6) for _ in range(100000)]
for n in [10, 100, 1000, 100000]:
    avg = sum(rolls[:n]) / n
    print(f"n = {n:>6}: average = {avg:.4f}, error = {abs(avg - 3.5):.4f}")  # prints: n = 10: average = 4.1000, error = 0.6000
```

In words: the error is $0.6$ at $n = 10$, $0.32$ at $n = 100$, $0.069$ at $n = 1000$ and $0.0101$ at $n = 100000$. It happens to fall at every printed size here, but it need not: with other seeds it wanders up and down at small $n$ and only shrinks overall. The rate matches the spread formula in the next section: the typical error scales like $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$.

## Why averages settle

The variance of a die roll is $\frac{35}{12}$. The sample mean of $n$ independent rolls has variance $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$, so its standard deviation is $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$. The script below checks this by simulation: it computes many averages of $n$ rolls and measures how much they spread.

```python
import math
import random
import statistics

random.seed(2024)
sigma = math.sqrt(35 / 12)
for n in [100, 1000]:
    means = [statistics.fmean(random.choices(range(1, 7), k=n)) for _ in range(2000)]
    print(f"n = {n:>4}: sd of 2000 averages = {statistics.stdev(means):.4f}, predicted sigma/sqrt(n) = {sigma / math.sqrt(n):.4f}")
# n =  100: sd of 2000 averages = 0.1699, predicted sigma/sqrt(n) = 0.1708
# n = 1000: sd of 2000 averages = 0.0543, predicted sigma/sqrt(n) = 0.0540
```

In words: the measured spread of the averages matches $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, about $0.17$ for a hundred rolls and about $0.054$ for a thousand. Each tenfold increase in sample size cuts the typical error by about $\sqrt{10}$, roughly three.

## What the law does not say

A common misreading is that the law forces outcomes to balance. Suppose a coin has landed heads ten times in a row. The law does not predict tails next. The coin has no memory, so the next flip is still fair. What changes with more flips is that the early streak becomes a smaller and smaller part of the average.

The check below simulates a million fair flips, finds every place where five heads in a row have just occurred, and looks at the flip that comes next.

```python
import random

random.seed(7)
flips = [random.random() < 0.5 for _ in range(1_000_000)]
after_streak = [flips[i] for i in range(5, len(flips)) if all(flips[i - 5:i])]
print("streaks of 5 heads found:", len(after_streak))
print("share of heads on the next flip:", round(sum(after_streak) / len(after_streak), 3))
# streaks of 5 heads found: 31444
# share of heads on the next flip: 0.502
```

In words: after tens of thousands of five-head streaks, the next flip is heads about half the time. Nothing pushes it toward tails. The average still settles, but only because the streak becomes a smaller and smaller part of an ever larger total.

<Callout type="pitfall">
The law of large numbers is about averages over many trials, not about compensation. Believing that a run of bad luck makes a good outcome more likely next is the gambler's fallacy. Independence means the next trial ignores the past, and the average moves only as more trials are added.
</Callout>

## Where it is used

The law justifies estimating a probability by the frequency of an event in repeated trials, and estimating an expected value by the sample average. Every simulation in this section depends on it: the average over many runs converges to the quantity the simulation is designed to measure.
