Prices, particle positions and many physical signals jitter at every scale. Ordinary calculus assumes smooth paths, so it cannot describe them directly. Stochastic calculus is the version built for paths that move randomly at all scales, and its central object is Brownian motion. This note introduces Brownian motion as the limit of a random walk, checks its scaling and quadratic variation by simulation, and states Itô's lemma with a check on a simple function.

## Brownian motion

Brownian motion is a continuous-time process $W_t$ with three properties: $W_0 = 0$, the increments $W_t - W_s$ over disjoint intervals are independent, and $W_t - W_s$ is normal with mean $0$ and variance $t - s$. It is the limit of a random walk with steps of size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ taken every $\Delta t$ time units. The scaling is chosen so the variance per unit time stays fixed.

The script below simulates $2000$ paths of $W_1$ with $1000$ steps each, using a seed, and checks that $W_1$ has mean $0$ and variance $1$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
steps, paths = 1000, 2000
dt = 1.0 / steps
increments = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(dt), size=(paths, steps))  # one row per path
W = increments.cumsum(axis=1)                                     # W at every time step
print("mean of W_1:", round(W[:, -1].mean(), 2), " variance of W_1:", round(W[:, -1].var(), 2))
print("variance of W_0.5:", round(W[:, steps // 2 - 1].var(), 2))
# mean of W_1: -0.03  variance of W_1: 0.96
# variance of W_0.5: 0.5
```

In words: the endpoint at time $1$ has mean near $0$ and variance near $1$, and halfway, at $t = 0.5$, the variance is about $0.5$: variance grows linearly in time, as the definition requires. Drawing all two million increments in one NumPy call takes a fraction of a second, where a pure-Python loop takes seconds. The small gaps are sampling error from $2000$ paths. The increments were drawn with standard deviation $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, which is what makes the total variance equal to $t$.

## Quadratic variation

The key feature of Brownian paths is that the sum of squared increments over $[0, 1]$ tends to $1$, even though the path length itself is infinite. Ordinary smooth functions have squared increments that vanish in the limit. Brownian motion keeps a fixed amount of squared variation at every scale.

```python
import math
import random

random.seed(3)
steps = 100000
dt = 1.0 / steps
qv = sum(random.gauss(0.0, math.sqrt(dt)) ** 2 for _ in range(steps))
print("sum of squared increments over [0, 1]:", round(qv, 3))  # prints: sum of squared increments over [0, 1]: 0.999
```

In words: a single path with a hundred thousand steps gives a total squared variation of $0.999$ to three places. This is why the rule $(dW)^2 = dt$ is used in the calculus: the squared increment is not negligible, it is of the same order as the time step.

## Itô's lemma

For an ordinary smooth function $f$, the chain rule says $df(x) = f'(x)\,dx$. For Brownian motion the second-order term survives because $(dW)^2 = dt$:

$$
df(W_t) = f'(W_t)\,dW_t + \frac{1}{2} f''(W_t)\,dt.
$$

Take $f(w) = w^2$. Then $f' = 2w$ and $f'' = 2$, and the lemma says $d(W_t^2) = 2W_t\,dW_t + dt$. Summed over $[0, 1]$, it says $W_1^2 = \sum 2W_{t_i}\,\Delta W_i + 1$ along a single path, where each term uses the value of $W$ at the start of its step. The ordinary chain rule would drop the $+1$. The check below adds up both sides on one simulated path.

```python
import math
import random

random.seed(3)
steps = 100000
dt = 1.0 / steps
w, ito_sum = 0.0, 0.0
for _ in range(steps):
    dw = random.gauss(0.0, math.sqrt(dt))
    ito_sum += 2 * w * dw   # f'(W) dW, with W taken at the start of the step
    w += dw
print("W_1^2:", round(w * w, 4), " sum of 2W dW:", round(ito_sum, 4), " difference:", round(w * w - ito_sum, 4))
# W_1^2: 0.3458  sum of 2W dW: -0.6534  difference: 0.9992
```

In words: the sum of $2W\,dW$ falls short of $W_1^2$ by about $1$, exactly the $\int_0^1 dt$ term Itô's lemma adds. The ordinary chain rule, $d(W^2) = 2W\,dW$, would predict a difference of $0$. Taking expectations also gives $E[W_t^2] = t$, because the $2W\,dW$ sum has mean zero.

<Callout type="pitfall">
Do not apply the ordinary chain rule to Brownian motion. Writing $d(W^2) = 2W\,dW$ drops the $dt$ term and gives the wrong expectation. Always use Itô's lemma, which includes the second derivative whenever the path has nonzero quadratic variation.
</Callout>

## Where it is used

Option pricing, interest-rate models and filtering all use stochastic differential equations of the form $dX_t = \mu(X_t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t)\,dW_t$. Itô's lemma is how you find the equation satisfied by a function of such a process, and it is the main technique behind the Black–Scholes option pricing model.
