Real numbers cannot solve $x^{2} = -1$. Rather than declare the problem impossible, mathematicians introduced a new number $i$ with $i^{2} = -1$ and found that the resulting system is more powerful than the reals. Every polynomial has a complex root, and rotation and oscillation become simple multiplication. This note introduces the arithmetic, the plane picture, polar form and Euler's formula. The [Number Systems](../number-systems/) note shows where $\mathbb{C}$ sits in the chain of extensions.

## Arithmetic

A complex number is written $z = a + bi$, with real $a$ (the real part, $\operatorname{Re} z$) and real $b$ (the imaginary part, $\operatorname{Im} z$). Arithmetic follows the usual rules, with $i^{2} = -1$ applied whenever it appears:

- $(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i$
- $(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$, since $i \cdot i = -1$
- The **conjugate** of $a + bi$ is $a - bi$, and $z \cdot \bar{z} = a^{2} + b^{2}$, a real number.

Division uses the conjugate: $1 / z = \bar{z} / (z \bar{z})$. This is how you divide by a complex number in practice.

## The complex plane

Identify $a + bi$ with the point $(a, b)$. The horizontal axis is the real line, the vertical axis is the imaginary line, and the plane is the **complex plane**. Addition is vector addition. The **modulus** $\lvert z\rvert= \sqrt{a^{2} + b^{2}}$ is the distance from the origin.

The **argument** $\arg z$ is the angle from the positive real axis to the point. Together, the modulus and the argument give the **polar form**:

$$
z = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta), \qquad r = \lvert z\rvert, \quad \theta = \arg z.

$$

Polar form explains multiplication. If $z = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta )$ and $w = s(\cos \varphi + i \sin \varphi )$, then $zw = rs(\cos(\theta + \varphi ) + i \sin(\theta + \varphi ))$. The lengths multiply and the angles add. Multiplying by $i$ rotates by 90°, since $i = 1(\cos 90^\circ + i \sin 90^\circ )$.

The script below converts $3 + 4i$ to polar form and checks that the modulus of a product is the product of the moduli.

```python
import cmath
import math

z = 3 + 4j
print("modulus of 3+4i:", abs(z))
r, theta = cmath.polar(z)
print(f"polar: r={r:.4f}, theta={theta:.4f} rad = {math.degrees(theta):.2f} deg")
print("back to rectangular:", cmath.rect(r, theta))

w = 1 + 1j
print("|z*w| == |z||w|:", math.isclose(abs(z * w), abs(z) * abs(w)))
# modulus of 3+4i: 5.0
# polar: r=5.0000, theta=0.9273 rad = 53.13 deg
# back to rectangular: (3.0000000000000004+3.9999999999999996j)
# |z*w| == |z||w|: True
```

In words: $3 + 4i$ has modulus 5 (the 3-4-5 triangle) and argument about 53°. Converting back gives $3 + 4i$ up to rounding in the last digit. The product test shows the modulus is multiplicative, which is the length half of the polar multiplication rule.

Python writes the imaginary unit as $j$, not $i$, and `1j` is a complex number while $1$ is an integer. A bare $i$ in code is a variable name, not the imaginary unit.
</Callout>

## Euler's formula

Euler showed that for every real $\theta$:

$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos \theta + i \sin \theta

$$

The left side is an exponential, defined by its power series. The right side is a point on the unit circle at angle $\theta$. So multiplying by $e^{i\theta }$ rotates the plane by $\theta$, and $e^{i\theta }$ is the unit circle. At $\theta = \pi$ the formula gives $e^{i\pi } = -1$, and rearranging gives $e^{i\pi } + 1 = 0$, a relation among five famous constants.

The script evaluates Euler's formula at $\pi$ and checks the cube roots of unity, the three points $e^{2\pi ik/3}$ that sit at 120° intervals on the unit circle.

```python
import cmath
import math

print("e^(i*pi) + 1 =", cmath.exp(1j * math.pi) + 1)

roots = [cmath.exp(2j * math.pi * k / 3) for k in range(3)]
print("cube roots of 1:", [(round(c.real, 3), round(c.imag, 3)) for c in roots])
print("each cubed is 1:", all(math.isclose(abs(c ** 3 - 1), 0, abs_tol=1e-9) for c in roots))
# e^(i*pi) + 1 = 1.2246467991473532e-16j
# cube roots of 1: [(1.0, 0.0), (-0.5, 0.866), (-0.5, -0.866)]
# each cubed is 1: True
```

In words: the sum is not exactly zero, because floating point cannot represent $\pi$ exactly, so the result is about $10^{-16}$ times $i$. That value is zero to machine precision and the identity holds. The three cube roots of 1 are the points at 0°, 120° and 240° on the circle, and each cubed returns 1.

## Roots of polynomials

The **fundamental theorem of algebra** says every non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root, and a degree-$n$ polynomial has exactly $n$ roots counted with multiplicity. Over the reals this fails: $x^{2} + 1$ has no real root. Working in $\mathbb{C}$ removes the exception, and it is why eigenvalues of real matrices are often complex, a point the Linear Algebra section returns to.

## Complex numbers in engineering

Alternating current, radio, and image processing all use complex numbers. A sinusoid $A \cos(\omega t + \varphi )$ is the real part of $A e^{i(\omega t + \varphi )}$, so a single complex number carries amplitude and phase together, and multiplying by $e^{i\varphi }$ shifts the phase. The Signals and Fourier section uses this to decompose signals into frequencies.
