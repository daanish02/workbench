| Uniform$(a, b)$ | $[a, b]$ | $\frac{1}{b-a}$ | $\frac{a+b}{2}$ | $\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}$ | every value in an interval equally likely | [Common distributions](/workbench/math/probability/common-distributions/) | `uniform(a, b - a)` |
| Exponential$(\lambda)$ | $[0, \infty)$ | $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ | $\frac{1}{\lambda}$ | $\frac{1}{\lambda^2}$ | waiting time with no memory | [Common distributions](/workbench/math/probability/common-distributions/) | `expon(scale=1/lam)` |
| Gamma$(k, \theta)$ | $(0, \infty)$ | $\frac{x^{k-1}e^{-x/\theta}}{\Gamma(k)\theta^k}$ | $k\theta$ | $k\theta^2$ | waiting time for the $k$-th Poisson event | [Common distributions](/workbench/math/probability/common-distributions/) | `gamma(k, scale=theta)` |
| Beta$(\alpha, \beta)$ | $[0, 1]$ | $\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}$ | $\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$ | $\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}$ | an uncertain probability; the conjugate prior of the binomial | [Bayesian inference](/workbench/math/statistics/bayesian-inference/) | `beta(a, b)` |
| Normal$(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\mathbb{R}$ | $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ | $\mu$ | $\sigma^2$ | sums and averages of many small effects | [Central limit theorem](/workbench/math/probability/central-limit-theorem/) | `norm(mu, sigma)` |
| Student $t_\nu$ | $\mathbb{R}$ | $\propto \left(1 + \frac{x^2}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+1)/2}$ | $0$ ($\nu > 1$) | $\frac{\nu}{\nu - 2}$ ($\nu > 2$) | a mean standardised by an estimated standard deviation | [Confidence intervals](/workbench/math/statistics/confidence-intervals/) | `t(nu)` |

## Useful facts

- About $68\%$ of a normal lies within one standard deviation of its mean, about $95\%$ within two, and about $99.7\%$ within three.
- The Poisson is the limit of the binomial when $n$ is large, $p$ is small, and $np = \lambda$.
- The exponential is the only continuous distribution with no memory: $P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)$.
- A sum of independent normals is normal, with means and variances added. A sum of independent Poissons is Poisson with rates added.

<Callout type="pitfall">
Do not read a density value as a probability. For the exponential with rate $5$, the density at $0$ is $5$, which is larger than $1$ and still legitimate; only areas under the density are probabilities.
</Callout>
