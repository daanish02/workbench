Most quantities depend on more than one thing. The temperature in a room depends on position and time; the output of a neural network depends on thousands of weights; the cost of a trip depends on distance and speed. Multivariable calculus extends differentiation and integration to these functions. The main new idea is the partial derivative: change one input at a time and see what happens. This note introduces partial derivatives, checks them numerically, and computes a double integral.

## Partial derivatives

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin y$. To find $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$, treat $y$ as a constant and differentiate in $x$: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy$. To find $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$, treat $x$ as a constant: $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + \cos y$.

Geometrically, the surface $z = f(x, y)$ has a tangent line in the $x$ direction and another in the $y$ direction, and the partial derivatives are their slopes. The same definition as a limit applies, with only one variable moving:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x_0, y_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h, y_0) - f(x_0, y_0)}{h}.

$$

The check below evaluates both partials at $(1.5, 0.8)$ by central differences and compares them with the formulas.

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda x, y: x ** 2 * y + np.sin(y)

x0, y0, h = 1.5, 0.8, 1e-6
fx = (f(x0 + h, y0) - f(x0 - h, y0)) / (2 * h)
fy = (f(x0, y0 + h) - f(x0, y0 - h)) / (2 * h)
print("f_x numeric:", round(fx, 6), " exact 2xy =", round(2 * x0 * y0, 6))  # prints: f_x numeric: 2.4  exact 2xy = 2.4
print("f_y numeric:", round(fy, 6), " exact x^2 + cos y =", round(x0 ** 2 + np.cos(y0), 6))  # prints: f_y numeric: 2.946707  exact x^2 + cos y = 2.946707
```

In words: both numerical partials agree with the formulas to six places. The $x$ partial $2.4$ is the slope of the surface in the $x$ direction at that point, and the $y$ partial $2.946707$ is the slope in the $y$ direction.

## Double integrals

The double integral $\iint_R f(x, y)\,dA$ sums $f$ over a region $R$. Over a rectangle $[a, b] \times [c, d]$ it is an iterated integral, one variable at a time: $\int_a^b \left(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\right) dx$, and the order can be swapped for nice functions (Fubini's theorem).

For $f(x, y) = xy$ over the unit square, the inner integral in $y$ is $\frac{x}{2}$ and the outer integral is $\int_0^1 \frac{x}{2}\,dx = \frac{1}{4}$. The check below computes the same double integral on a grid.

```python
import numpy as np

n = 2000
xs = np.linspace(0, 1, n)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, xs)
val = np.trapezoid(np.trapezoid(X * Y, xs, axis=1), xs)
print("double integral of xy over unit square:", round(val, 6), " exact 1/4 =", 0.25)  # prints: double integral of xy over unit square: 0.25  exact 1/4 = 0.25
```

In words: the grid computation gives $0.25$, matching $\frac{1}{4}$. The code integrates along one axis, then the other, exactly as the iterated integral does on paper.

<Callout type="pitfall">
A partial derivative is not the total rate of change. If $x$ and $y$ both depend on time, the total change is $\frac{df}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$. The next notes develop this as the multivariable chain rule.
</Callout>

## Where it is used

Physics describes fields such as temperature and pressure with partial derivatives, and conservation laws are equations involving them. Economics uses partial derivatives for how profit responds to each input. Machine learning uses the gradient, the vector of all partial derivatives, to decide how to change each weight.
