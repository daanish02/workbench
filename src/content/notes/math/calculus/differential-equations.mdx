Many laws of nature do not say what a quantity is; they say how it changes. Population grows in proportion to its size. Radioactive material decays in proportion to what is left. A cooling cup of tea loses heat in proportion to the temperature difference. Each of these is a differential equation, and solving it means finding the function whose rate of change matches the rule. This note covers the cases you can solve exactly, and Euler's method for the cases you cannot.

## The exponential equation

The simplest example is $y' = ky$ with $y(0) = y_0$. The solution is $y(t) = y_0 e^{kt}$. To check, differentiate: $y' = k y_0 e^{kt} = k y$, which is the equation, and $y(0) = y_0$ is the initial condition. Growth and decay are the same equation with the sign of $k$ reversed.

The script below solves $y' = -2y$ with $y(0) = 1$ and approximates $y(1)$ by Euler's method with increasing numbers of steps. The exact answer is $e^{-2}$.

```python
import math

def euler(f, y0, t_end, steps):
    h = t_end / steps
    y = y0
    for _ in range(steps):
        y = y + h * f(y)
    return y

f = lambda y: -2 * y
exact = math.exp(-2 * 1.0)
for steps in [10, 100, 1000]:
    approx = euler(f, 1.0, 1.0, steps)
    print(f"Euler with {steps:>4} steps: y(1) = {approx:.6f}, error {abs(approx - exact):.2e}")
print("exact y(1) = e^-2 =", round(exact, 6))
# Euler with   10 steps: y(1) = 0.107374, error 2.80e-02
# Euler with  100 steps: y(1) = 0.132620, error 2.72e-03
# Euler with 1000 steps: y(1) = 0.135065, error 2.71e-04
# exact y(1) = e^-2 = 0.135335
```

In words: with $10$ steps the approximation is off by $0.028$, with $100$ by $0.0027$, and with $1000$ by $0.00027$. The error falls roughly tenfold for each tenfold increase in steps. Euler's method is first-order accurate, so it is slow, but it works for equations with no closed-form solution.

## Separable equations

A first-order equation is separable when it can be written $\frac{dy}{dt} = g(t)\,h(y)$. Divide by $h(y)$, multiply by $dt$, and integrate both sides:

$$
\int \frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(t)\,dt.

$$

For $y' = y^2$ with $y(0) = 1$, the separated form is $\frac{dy}{y^2} = dt$, so $-\frac{1}{y} = t + C$. The initial condition gives $C = -1$, and so $y = \frac{1}{1 - t}$. The solution blows up at $t = 1$, which the derivative of $y$ also reveals: a solution can cease to exist in finite time.

The check below evaluates that solution at $t = 0.5$, where the formula gives $2$.

```python
def euler(f, y0, t_end, steps):
    h, y = t_end / steps, y0
    for _ in range(steps):
        y = y + h * f(y)
    return y

for t_end in (0.5, 0.9, 0.99):
    print(f"t = {t_end}: Euler (10000 steps) = {euler(lambda y: y * y, 1.0, t_end, 10000):.3f}, exact 1/(1 - t) = {1 / (1 - t_end):.3f}")
# t = 0.5: Euler (10000 steps) = 2.000, exact 1/(1 - t) = 2.000
# t = 0.9: Euler (10000 steps) = 9.979, exact 1/(1 - t) = 10.000
# t = 0.99: Euler (10000 steps) = 95.684, exact 1/(1 - t) = 100.000
```

In words: the numerical solution follows $\frac{1}{1 - t}$ and climbs ever faster as $t$ approaches $1$. Between $t = 0$ and $t = 1$ the solution grows from $1$ to infinity, so the equation has no solution beyond $t = 1$, and a numerical solver falls further behind the closer it gets.

<Callout type="pitfall">
When you divide by $h(y)$, you may lose solutions where $h(y) = 0$. For $y' = y^2$, the constant solution $y = 0$ satisfies the equation but is not in the family $y = \frac{1}{1-t}$. Check for such constant solutions before you finish.
</Callout>

## Stability of Euler's method

A small step is not only about accuracy. For $y' = ky$ with $k < 0$, one Euler step multiplies $y$ by $1 + hk$. The true solution decays, but the numbers decay only when $|1 + hk| < 1$, that is $h < 2/|k|$. With a larger step the method oscillates and blows up, however simple the equation.

```python
def euler(k, h, steps, y=1.0):
    for _ in range(steps):
        y = y + h * k * y          # each step multiplies y by (1 + h k)
    return y

for h in (0.1, 0.9, 1.1):
    print(f"h = {h}: factor 1 + hk = {1 - 2 * h:+.1f}, y after 20 steps = {euler(-2, h, 20):.3g}")
# h = 0.1: factor 1 + hk = +0.8, y after 20 steps = 0.0115
# h = 0.9: factor 1 + hk = -0.8, y after 20 steps = 0.0115
# h = 1.1: factor 1 + hk = -1.2, y after 20 steps = 38.3
```

In words: for $y' = -2y$ the limit is $h < 1$. At $h = 0.9$ the iterates flip sign every step but still shrink; at $h = 1.1$ they grow without bound, although the true solution decays to zero. This is the same condition as the step-size limit $\eta < 2/a$ in the [Gradient Descent](../../optimization/gradient-descent/) note, because gradient descent is Euler's method on the flow $\mathbf{x}' = -\nabla f(\mathbf{x})$.

## Systems and second-order equations

Most models have several interacting quantities. A linear system $\mathbf{y}' = A\mathbf{y}$ is solved by the [eigenvalues](../../linear-algebra/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors/) of $A$: along each eigenvector the solution is $e^{\lambda t}$ times that vector, so negative real parts decay, positive ones grow, and complex pairs oscillate. A second-order equation becomes a system by naming the derivative: the oscillator $y'' = -\omega^2 y$ is $\mathbf{y} = (y, y')$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, whose eigenvalues $\pm i\omega$ give $\cos \omega t$ and $\sin \omega t$.

In practice a library solver chooses the steps adaptively and uses higher-order formulas:

```python
import numpy as np
import sympy as sp
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, y: [y[1], -4 * y[0]], (0, np.pi), [1.0, 0.0], rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12)
print("y(pi) for y'' = -4y, y(0) = 1:", round(sol.y[0, -1], 6), " exact cos(2 pi) = 1")
print("eigenvalues of A:", np.linalg.eigvals([[0, 1], [-4, 0]]))

t = sp.symbols("t")
y = sp.Function("y")
print(sp.dsolve(sp.Eq(y(t).diff(t), y(t) ** 2), ics={y(0): 1}))
# y(pi) for y'' = -4y, y(0) = 1: 1.0  exact cos(2 pi) = 1
# eigenvalues of A: [0.+2.j 0.-2.j]
# Eq(y(t), -1/(t - 1))
```

In words: `solve_ivp` (an adaptive Runge–Kutta method by default) follows the oscillation through a full period to $y(\pi) = 1$, the eigenvalues $\pm 2i$ explain the frequency $2$, and SymPy's `dsolve` reproduces the separable solution $y = \frac{1}{1 - t}$ found by hand above. Run it with `uv run --with numpy --with scipy --with sympy`.

## Why numerical methods matter

Most differential equations have no closed-form solution. Engineers solve them with Euler's method or its more accurate relatives, such as the Runge–Kutta family inside `solve_ivp`, which trade computing time for precision. Step size controls both the error and, as shown above, the stability.

## Where differential equations are used

Circuit analysis, mechanical vibrations, heat flow, population dynamics, and the training of neural networks viewed as continuous flows are all differential equations. The equations in this note are the simplest members of a large family, and most of physics is written in that family.
