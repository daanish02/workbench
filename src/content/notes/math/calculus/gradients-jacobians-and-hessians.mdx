A function of several variables has a single number at each point, but its behaviour is captured by a set of arrays. The gradient is the vector of first partial derivatives; it tells you which direction is uphill. The Jacobian generalises that to functions with several outputs. The Hessian is the matrix of second derivatives, which describes the curvature. Together they are the toolkit for optimisation, and for the training of neural networks, which is nothing more than optimisation of a very large function.

## The gradient

For $f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ the gradient collects the partial derivatives into a vector, written $\nabla f$. For $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$ it is $\nabla f = (2x, 6y)$.

Its key property is geometric. The directional derivative in a unit direction $\mathbf{u}$ is $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$, and by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality this is largest when $\mathbf{u}$ points along $\nabla f$. So the gradient is the direction of steepest ascent, and the negative gradient is the direction of steepest descent. The check below confirms the gradient numerically and that stepping along it raises the function.

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda p: p[0] ** 2 + 3 * p[1] ** 2

def grad_num(f, p, h=1e-6):
    g = np.zeros(2)
    for i in range(2):
        e = np.zeros(2)
        e[i] = h
        g[i] = (f(p + e) - f(p - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g

p = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
print("gradient numeric:", np.round(grad_num(f, p), 4), " exact (2x, 6y) =", [2, 12])  # prints: gradient numeric: [ 2. 12.]  exact (2x, 6y) = [2, 12]

step = 0.01 * grad_num(f, p) / np.linalg.norm(grad_num(f, p))
print("f increases along gradient:", f(p + step) > f(p))  # prints: f increases along gradient: True
```

In words: the gradient at $(1, 2)$ is $(2, 12)$, which points mostly in the $y$ direction because $f$ is steeper there. A small step along it increases $f$, as the geometry predicts.

## The Jacobian

When $\mathbf{F}$ maps $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$, its derivative is an $m \times n$ matrix, the Jacobian, whose entries are $J_{ij} = \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}$. Each row is the gradient of one output. Near a point, $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{h}) \approx \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{p}) + J\mathbf{h}$: the Jacobian is the linear map that best approximates $\mathbf{F}$.

For $\mathbf{F}(x, y) = (xy, x + y^2)$ the Jacobian is

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} y & x \\ 1 & 2y \end{pmatrix}.

$$

At $(1, 2)$ this is $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. The check below computes it by finite differences and confirms the formula at that point.

```python
import numpy as np

F = lambda p: np.array([p[0] * p[1], p[0] + p[1] ** 2])
p = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
J = np.zeros((2, 2))
h = 1e-6
for j in range(2):
    e = np.zeros(2)
    e[j] = h
    J[:, j] = (F(p + e) - F(p - e)) / (2 * h)
print("Jacobian numeric:\n", np.round(J, 4))  # prints: Jacobian numeric: [[2. 1.] [1. 4.]]
```

In words: the numerical Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, matching the formula at $(1, 2)$. Each row holds the gradient of one output component.

## The Hessian and curvature

The Hessian of a scalar function is the symmetric matrix $H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$. At a critical point it classifies the point through its eigenvalues. All positive means a local minimum, all negative a local maximum, and mixed signs a saddle point.

For $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$ the Hessian is the constant matrix $\operatorname{diag}(2, 6)$. Its eigenvalues are $2$ and $6$, both positive, so $f$ is a bowl with a single minimum at the origin. The check below computes them.

```python
import numpy as np
import sympy as sp

x, y = sp.symbols("x y")
H = sp.hessian(x**2 + 3 * y**2, (x, y))           # second partial derivatives, computed symbolically
print("Hessian:", H.tolist())                      # prints: Hessian: [[2, 0], [0, 6]]
print("Hessian eigenvalues:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.array(H, dtype=float)))  # prints: Hessian eigenvalues: [2. 6.]
```

In words: both eigenvalues are positive, so the bowl curves upward in every direction. The eigenvalue $6$ is larger than $2$, so the function is more sharply curved in the $y$ direction. That steep direction caps the learning rate: [gradient descent](../../optimization/gradient-descent/) is stable only for $\eta < 2/6$, and at such a rate the flatter $x$ direction, with curvature $2$, converges slowly.

<Callout type="pitfall">
The gradient of a function with several outputs is not defined; the Jacobian is the right object. Writing "the gradient of $\mathbf{F}$" and meaning its Jacobian is common, but the two differ when the output is a vector.
</Callout>

The test is the positive-definiteness test of the [Quadratic Forms](../../linear-algebra/quadratic-forms/) note: near a critical point $f(\mathbf{x}_0 + \mathbf{h}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}_0) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{h}^\top H\mathbf{h}$, and the figure there shows how the eigenvalues of $H$ turn that quadratic into a bowl, a saddle or a ridge.

## Why this matters for training

Training a neural network minimises a loss $L(\mathbf{w})$ over millions of weights. The gradient $\nabla L$ says how to change every weight, and gradient descent repeats $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta \nabla L$. The Hessian's eigenvalues explain why learning rates must be small in steep directions and why training can crawl along flat ones. Computing the Jacobian of each layer, and chaining them, is what backpropagation does.
