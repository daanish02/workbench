The derivative is a tool for finding the best value of something: the largest area that fits a fence, the smallest cost, the highest point of a trajectory. Each of those is a function of one variable, and the best value sits where its rate of change is zero. This note shows how to find those points, how to tell a maximum from a minimum, and how the first and second derivatives together sketch a curve. The method applies directly to the optimisation that trains machine learning models.

## Critical points

A **critical point** of $f$ is a value $c$ where $f'(c) = 0$, or where $f'$ does not exist. At a smooth interior maximum or minimum the curve is flat, so the derivative vanishes there. The converse is not true: a flat point can be neither a maximum nor a minimum, as in $f(x) = x^3$ at $x = 0$.

To find the maximum of a function on a closed interval, check the critical points inside it and the two endpoints, then take the largest value.

## The mean value theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable inside, some point $c$ between them has

$$
f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.

$$

In words: somewhere the instantaneous slope equals the average slope. If a car covers $100$ km in one hour, at some moment the speedometer read exactly $100$ km/h. The theorem is what turns derivative facts into function facts: if $f' > 0$ everywhere on an interval, any two points have $f(b) - f(a) = f'(c)(b - a) > 0$, so $f$ is increasing.

## An optimisation problem

Suppose you have $20$ metres of fence and want the rectangle with the largest area. Call the width $x$. The length is then $10 - x$, since the perimeter is $2x + 2(10 - x) = 20$. The area is

$$
A(x) = x(10 - x) = 10x - x^2.

$$

The derivative is $A'(x) = 10 - 2x$, which is zero at $x = 5$. The second derivative $A''(x) = -2$ is negative, so $x = 5$ is a maximum. The rectangle is a square of side $5$, with area $25$.

The check below evaluates the area on a fine grid and reads off the largest value. It should agree with the calculus.

```python
import numpy as np

xs = np.linspace(0, 10, 10001)
area = xs * (10 - xs)
i = np.argmax(area)
print("best width:", round(xs[i], 3), "area:", round(area[i], 3))
print("derivative at x = 5:", 10 - 2 * 5)
# best width: 5.0 area: 25.0
# derivative at x = 5: 0
```

In words: the grid search finds a width of $5.0$ and an area of $25.0$, matching the derivative. Checking the derivative at that point gives $0$, which is the condition for a critical point. A grid is a reliable check, but calculus gives the exact answer and says why the point is a maximum.

## Sketching a curve

Take $f(x) = x^3 - 3x$. Its derivative $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3$ vanishes at $x = \pm 1$. The second derivative $f''(x) = 6x$ classifies them: negative at $x = -1$, so a local maximum, and positive at $x = 1$, so a local minimum.

```python
import numpy as np

f = lambda x: x ** 3 - 3 * x
roots = np.roots([3, 0, -3])  # solves 3x^2 - 3 = 0
for r in sorted(roots.real):
    second = 6 * r
    kind = "local max" if second < 0 else "local min"
    print(f"x = {r:.4f}: f = {f(r):.4f}, f'' = {second:.4f} -> {kind}")
# x = -1.0000: f = 2.0000, f'' = -6.0000 -> local max
# x = 1.0000: f = -2.0000, f'' = 6.0000 -> local min
```

In words: at $x = -1$ the curve peaks at height $2$, and at $x = 1$ it dips to $-2$. Between them the curve falls, and outside them it rises. Those four facts sketch the cubic: it rises, peaks, falls through the middle, dips, and rises again.

<Callout type="pitfall">
A zero of $f'$ does not guarantee an extremum. At $x = 0$ the function $x^3$ has $f'(0) = 0$ and $f''(0) = 0$, and it is increasing straight through. When the second derivative test is inconclusive, look at the sign of $f'$ on either side instead.
</Callout>

## Limits by derivatives: L'Hôpital's rule

When a limit has the form $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$, and $f$ and $g$ are differentiable near $a$, **L'Hôpital's rule** says

$$
\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}

$$

whenever the right side exists. For $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ at $0$ it gives $\frac{\cos 0}{1} = 1$, and for $\frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2}$ it needs two rounds:

```python
import sympy as sp

x = sp.symbols("x")
f, g = sp.exp(x) - 1 - x, x**2
print("f/g at 0 is 0/0:", f.subs(x, 0), g.subs(x, 0))
print("after one round:", sp.diff(f, x) / sp.diff(g, x), " still 0/0")
print("after two rounds:", sp.diff(f, x, 2) / sp.diff(g, x, 2), "->", sp.limit(f / g, x, 0))
# f/g at 0 is 0/0: 0 0
# after one round: (exp(x) - 1)/(2*x)  still 0/0
# after two rounds: exp(x)/2 -> 1/2
```

In words: the ratio is $\frac{0}{0}$ at $0$, still $\frac{0}{0}$ after one differentiation, and $\frac{e^x}{2} \to \frac{1}{2}$ after two, which SymPy's `limit` confirms. The answer matches the Taylor view: $e^x - 1 - x \approx \frac{x^2}{2}$ near $0$.

<Callout type="pitfall">
L'Hôpital's rule applies only to $\frac{0}{0}$ and $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ forms. Applied to $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x + 1}{x + 2} = \frac{1}{2}$, it would give the wrong answer $1$. Check the form before differentiating.
</Callout>

## Related rates

When two quantities are linked, their rates of change are linked too. For a sphere, $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, so $\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2 \frac{dr}{dt}$. The chain rule turns a known rate of the radius into the rate of volume. This is the same composition of functions as in the [Derivatives](../derivatives/) note, now with time as the outer variable.

## Where this is used

Gradient descent is optimisation by the derivative: step against the slope until it is flat. Engineers use optimisation to minimise material or cost, and physicists find the path of least action as a critical point of an integral.
