Suppose you want the largest rectangle with a fixed perimeter, or the point on a curve nearest a target. The objective and the constraint are both smooth, and at the best point they must be aligned in a particular way. Lagrange multipliers turn that alignment into equations you can solve. The idea is geometric, and the algebra is short. This note states the condition, works two examples, and explains what the multiplier means.

## The geometric condition

Level curves of $f$ are the sets where $f$ is constant, and the gradient $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to them. The constraint $g(x) = 0$ is itself a level set of $g$. Moving along the constraint cannot change $f$ at a constrained optimum, so the gradient of $f$ must be perpendicular to the constraint curve. That makes it parallel to $\nabla g$:

$$
\nabla f(x) = \lambda\,\nabla g(x), \qquad g(x) = 0.
$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is the Lagrange multiplier. Equivalently, the point is a stationary point of the Lagrangian $L(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda g(x)$.

## Example: maximise a product on a line

Maximise $xy$ subject to $x + y = 10$. The gradients are $\nabla f = (y, x)$ and $\nabla g = (1, 1)$. Parallel means $y = \lambda$ and $x = \lambda$, so $x = y$, and the constraint then gives $x = y = 5$ with $\lambda = 5$. The check below searches the line for the largest product.

```python
best = max(((x / 100, 10 - x / 100) for x in range(0, 1001)), key=lambda p: p[0] * p[1])
print("best point on x + y = 10:", best, " product:", round(best[0] * best[1], 4))  # prints: best point on x + y = 10: (5.0, 5.0) product: 25.0
```

In words: the search confirms $(5, 5)$ with product $25$. The multiplier $\lambda = 5$ says that enlarging the constraint total by one unit would raise the best product by about $5$. That is what $\lambda$ measures.

## Example: nearest point on a circle

Find the point on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ nearest to $(3, 1)$. Minimising the squared distance $(x - 3)^2 + (y - 1)^2$ with the constraint $g = x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0$, the condition gives $2(x - 3) = 2\lambda x$ and $2(y - 1) = 2\lambda y$. Solving these with the circle equation gives the nearest point along the ray from the origin through $(3, 1)$.

```python
import math

target = (3.0, 1.0)
pts = [(math.cos(t / 10000 * 2 * math.pi), math.sin(t / 10000 * 2 * math.pi)) for t in range(10000)]
best = min(pts, key=lambda p: (p[0] - target[0]) ** 2 + (p[1] - target[1]) ** 2)
ray = (target[0] / math.hypot(*target), target[1] / math.hypot(*target))
print("grid nearest point:", tuple(round(c, 3) for c in best), " ray direction:", tuple(round(c, 3) for c in ray))  # prints: grid nearest point: (0.949, 0.316) ray direction: (0.949, 0.316)
```

In words: the nearest point on the circle lies on the line from the origin to the target, which the gradient condition predicts. The grid search and the ray direction agree to three decimals.

Solving the Lagrange equations symbolically shows the pitfall below in action: they have two solutions.

```python
import sympy as sp

x, y, lam = sp.symbols("x y lambda", real=True)
eqs = [2 * (x - 3) - 2 * lam * x, 2 * (y - 1) - 2 * lam * y, x**2 + y**2 - 1]
for s in sp.solve(eqs, [x, y, lam], dict=True):
    d2 = (s[x] - 3) ** 2 + (s[y] - 1) ** 2
    print(f"x = {float(s[x]):+.4f}, y = {float(s[y]):+.4f}, squared distance {float(d2):.4f}")
# x = +0.9487, y = +0.3162, squared distance 4.6754
# x = -0.9487, y = -0.3162, squared distance 17.3246
```

In words: the conditions give the point $(0.949, 0.316)$ toward the target, at squared distance about $4.68$, and the opposite point $(-0.949, -0.316)$, at about $17.32$, the farthest point on the circle. Both are stationary points of the Lagrangian; comparing values picks the minimum. Run it with `uv run --with sympy`.

<Callout type="pitfall">
The Lagrange conditions are necessary, not sufficient. Solving them gives stationary points that may be maxima, minima or saddles, and some constraint surfaces have points where the constraint gradient vanishes, which the method does not handle. Evaluate every candidate and check the constraint gradient is nonzero at it.
</Callout>

The answer, $x = y = \sqrt{c}$, is the product example turned around: there the sum was fixed and the product maximised, here the product is fixed and the sum minimised, and both put the optimum where the two gradients are parallel.

## Several constraints

With several equality constraints $g_i(x) = 0$, each gets its own multiplier and the condition becomes $\nabla f = \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i$. Inequality constraints need the extra conditions of the KKT theorem, which the [Duality](../duality/) note states.
