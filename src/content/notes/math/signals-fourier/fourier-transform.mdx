The [Fourier Series](../fourier-series/) handles signals that repeat. Most real signals do not, such as a single spoken word or a pulse from a sensor. The Fourier transform extends the idea: instead of a discrete set of harmonics at multiples of a base frequency, it uses a continuum of frequencies. This note defines the transform, works out two examples that recur everywhere, and states the properties that make it useful: shifting changes only phase, and squeezing in time spreads in frequency.

## From series to transform

A Fourier series of period $T$ uses the frequencies $k/T$. As $T$ grows the spacing $1/T$ shrinks, and in the limit every frequency is allowed. The coefficient of frequency $\xi$ becomes

$$
\hat f(\xi) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-2\pi i \xi t}\,dt, \qquad f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \hat f(\xi)\,e^{2\pi i \xi t}\,d\xi.

$$

In words: multiply the signal by a pure wave of frequency $\xi$ and add up the result; a large value means the signal contains that frequency. The second formula rebuilds the signal from its spectrum, so nothing is lost. Written with $\xi$ in cycles per unit time, as here, the two formulas are symmetric; with angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi\xi$ a factor $\frac{1}{2\pi}$ appears in one of them.

## Two examples

A **rectangular pulse** equal to $1$ on $[-\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}]$ and $0$ elsewhere has

$$
\hat f(\xi) = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} e^{-2\pi i \xi t}\,dt = \frac{\sin \pi\xi}{\pi\xi},

$$

the **sinc** function: a central lobe and side lobes that die away slowly, because the pulse has sharp edges. A **Gaussian** $e^{-\pi t^2}$ is its own transform, $\hat f(\xi) = e^{-\pi \xi^2}$: a smooth signal with no edges has a smooth, fast-decaying spectrum.

```python
import math
from scipy.integrate import quad

def transform(f, xi, lim):
    # the signals here are even, so the imaginary part vanishes and only the cosine term remains
    return quad(lambda t: f(t) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * xi * t), -lim, lim, limit=200)[0]

pulse = lambda t: 1.0 if abs(t) <= 0.5 else 0.0
gauss = lambda t: math.exp(-math.pi * t * t)
for xi in (0.0, 0.5, 1.5):
    sinc = 1.0 if xi == 0 else math.sin(math.pi * xi) / (math.pi * xi)
    print(f"xi = {xi}: pulse {transform(pulse, xi, 0.5):+.5f} (sinc {sinc:+.5f}), "
          f"Gaussian {transform(gauss, xi, 8):.5f} (exp(-pi xi^2) {math.exp(-math.pi * xi * xi):.5f})")
# xi = 0.0: pulse +1.00000 (sinc +1.00000), Gaussian 1.00000 (exp(-pi xi^2) 1.00000)
# xi = 0.5: pulse +0.63662 (sinc +0.63662), Gaussian 0.45594 (exp(-pi xi^2) 0.45594)
# xi = 1.5: pulse -0.21221 (sinc -0.21221), Gaussian 0.00085 (exp(-pi xi^2) 0.00085)
```

In words: the numerical integrals reproduce the sinc values, including the negative side lobe at $\xi = 1.5$, and the Gaussian values, which fall off far faster. Run it with `uv run --with scipy`.

## Shifting and scaling

Two properties do most of the work in practice.

- **Shift.** Delaying a signal by $t_0$ multiplies its transform by $e^{-2\pi i \xi t_0}$. The magnitude $|\hat f(\xi)|$ is unchanged, so a spectrum analyser cannot tell when a sound started, only what it contains.
- **Scaling.** Squeezing a signal in time, $f(at)$, gives $\frac{1}{|a|}\hat f(\xi / a)$: the spectrum stretches by the same factor. A pulse half as long has a central lobe twice as wide.

The scaling property is the **time–bandwidth trade-off**: a signal cannot be both short in time and narrow in frequency. Its sharpest form is the uncertainty principle, $\sigma_t\,\sigma_\xi \ge \frac{1}{4\pi}$ for the spreads of $|f|^2$ and $|\hat f|^2$, with equality exactly for Gaussians. That is why a short click sounds like every frequency at once, and why identifying a low note needs a longer listening window than a high one.

<Callout type="pitfall">
Conventions differ. Some texts use angular frequency $\omega$ and put $\frac{1}{2\pi}$ on the inverse, others split it as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ on both sides, and the sign of the exponent flips between physics and engineering. Check the convention before comparing formulas or numbers from two sources.
</Callout>

## Why the frequency domain is useful

In the frequency domain, filtering becomes multiplication, differentiation becomes multiplication by $2\pi i\xi$, and convolution becomes a product, as the [Convolution](../convolution/) note shows. A computer samples a signal first, which the [Sampling Theorem](../sampling/) note covers, and then uses the discrete version of the transform in the [DFT and FFT](../dft-and-fft/) note.
