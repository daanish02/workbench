[RAID](../raid/) showed how to survive a drive failure. This note asks how often drives actually fail, and in what ways, using real fleet statistics. Whole-drive failure is only one mode: drives also fail to read individual sectors, and occasionally return wrong data without any error at all. Each mode needs a different defence, and some threats (a mistaken delete, ransomware, a fire) are not drive failures at all. The note covers **failure rates**, **unrecoverable read errors** and why large RAID 5 rebuilds are risky, **silent corruption** with checksums and scrubbing, SMART monitoring, and ends with backups: the **3-2-1-1-0** rule and recovery objectives.

## How Often Drives Fail

The most useful public data comes from Backblaze, a backup company that publishes failure statistics for its fleet. Failure rates are reported as an **annualized failure rate (AFR)**: failures divided by drive-years of operation.

```python
drive_days, failures = 115_638_676, 4_317         # Backblaze Drive Stats, full year 2025
afr = failures / (drive_days / 365)               # failures per drive-year
print(f"annualized failure rate: {afr:.2%}")
for fleet in (12, 1_000, 300_000):
    print(f"{fleet:>7,} drives: about {fleet * afr:8,.1f} failures a year ({fleet * afr / 365:.2f} a day)")
# annualized failure rate: 1.36%
#      12 drives: about      0.2 failures a year (0.00 a day)
#   1,000 drives: about     13.6 failures a year (0.04 a day)
# 300,000 drives: about  4,087.8 failures a year (11.20 a day)
```

In words: across 115.6 million drive-days in 2025, 4,317 drives failed, an AFR of 1.36%. A home NAS with 12 drives sees a failure every few years; a company with 1,000 drives replaces about 14 a year; a fleet of 300,000 replaces about 11 every day, so at scale failure is routine and replacement is a scheduled job. Rates vary several-fold between drive models and rise with age, and failures cluster in time: drives bought together, from one batch, running in one enclosure, tend to fail together.

## Unreadable Sectors

A drive can be healthy and still fail to read one sector: the error-correcting code cannot recover it, and the read returns an error. Datasheets quote the **unrecoverable read error rate (URE)**, often 1 per $10^{14}$ bits for consumer drives and 1 per $10^{15}$ for enterprise drives. That matters during a RAID 5 rebuild, which must read every surviving drive in full and has no redundancy left to fix a bad sector:

```python
import math

read_tb = 7 * 20                                  # RAID 5 rebuild of eight 20 TB drives reads the other seven
bits = read_tb * 1e12 * 8
for spec, rate in [("consumer, 1 per 1e14 bits", 1e-14), ("enterprise, 1 per 1e15 bits", 1e-15)]:
    clean = math.exp(-bits * rate)               # chance of reading everything without one unreadable sector
    print(f"{spec}: {clean:.4%} chance the rebuild reads all {read_tb} TB cleanly")

afr, days, others = 0.0136, 2, 7                  # a second drive failing during a 2-day rebuild
p_second = 1 - (1 - afr * days / 365) ** others
print(f"second whole-drive failure during the rebuild: {p_second:.3%}")
# consumer, 1 per 1e14 bits: 0.0014% chance the rebuild reads all 140 TB cleanly
# enterprise, 1 per 1e15 bits: 32.6280% chance the rebuild reads all 140 TB cleanly
# second whole-drive failure during the rebuild: 0.052%
```

In words: if drives really failed one read in $10^{14}$ bits, reading 140 TB would almost certainly hit an unreadable sector (0.0014% chance of a clean pass); at the enterprise figure the chance of a clean rebuild is still only 32.6%. A second whole-drive failure during a two-day rebuild is far less likely, 0.052%, assuming independence. The datasheet URE is a guaranteed upper bound and real drives usually do much better, but the conclusion is why large arrays use RAID 6 or erasure coding: a second parity can repair the unreadable sector that RAID 5 cannot. A good RAID implementation also loses only the affected stripe, not the whole array.

## Silent Corruption

Worse than an error is wrong data with no error. Firmware bugs, misdirected writes (data written to the wrong sector), bit flips in RAM or on a cable, and flash cells that drift can all return a block that reads fine but is not what was written. RAID alone cannot tell which copy is right: a mirror with two different blocks gives no hint which one is correct.

The defence is **end-to-end checksums**: the [file system](../../../cs/operating-systems/file-systems/) (ZFS, Btrfs) or application stores a checksum of each block separately from the block, verifies it on every read, and on a mismatch reads the redundant copy and repairs the bad one. **Scrubbing** reads every block periodically (often monthly) to find latent errors while redundancy still exists, instead of discovering them during a rebuild.

**SMART** (self-monitoring, analysis and reporting technology) counters such as reallocated sectors and pending sectors give some warning: drives with rising counts are much more likely to fail. They are not reliable alone: in Google's 2007 study of over 100,000 drives, more than half of the failed drives showed no warning in the strongest SMART signals.

## RAID Is Not a Backup

RAID, [replication](../../../cs/distributed-systems/replication/) and snapshots on the same system protect **availability**: the service keeps running when hardware fails. They do not protect the **data** against most of the ways it is actually lost, because every redundant copy faithfully receives the same change:

- a user or script **deletes** or overwrites files: the deletion is mirrored instantly;
- a **software bug** writes corrupt data: the corruption is replicated;
- **ransomware** encrypts everything the compromised account can reach, including network shares and synced cloud folders;
- a **fire, flood or theft** takes every copy in the building.

A **backup** is a separate copy that these events cannot reach at the same time. The widely used rule, extended from the original 3-2-1:

- **3** copies of the data (the original plus two backups),
- on **2** different kinds of media or systems,
- **1** copy offsite,
- **1** copy offline, air-gapped or **immutable** (write-once storage that even an administrator cannot delete before a set date),
- **0** errors when restores are tested.

The same reasoning in numbers, with illustrative yearly probabilities:

```python
p_device = 0.02                                   # chance per year that one copy is lost to its own device or mistake
p_site = 0.001                                    # chance per year of losing a whole site (fire, flood, theft)
p_ransom = 0.01                                   # chance per year that ransomware encrypts everything it can reach

def loss(copies_same_site, offsite, offline):
    independent = p_device ** (copies_same_site + offsite + offline)
    site = p_site if offsite + offline == 0 else 0           # one disaster takes every copy at that site
    ransom = p_ransom if offline == 0 else 0                 # online copies are all reachable by the attacker
    return 1 - (1 - independent) * (1 - site) * (1 - ransom)

for label, args in [("3 copies, one site, all online", (3, 0, 0)), ("2 on site + 1 offsite, online", (2, 1, 0)), ("2 on site + 1 offsite + 1 offline", (2, 1, 1))]:
    print(f"{label:34} chance of losing everything in a year: {loss(*args):.1e}")
# 3 copies, one site, all online     chance of losing everything in a year: 1.1e-02
# 2 on site + 1 offsite, online      chance of losing everything in a year: 1.0e-02
# 2 on site + 1 offsite + 1 offline  chance of losing everything in a year: 1.6e-07
```

In words: three copies that fail independently would almost never all be lost (0.02 cubed is 8 in a million), but with all of them online at one site, the shared threats dominate and the yearly risk is 1.1%. Moving one copy offsite removes the fire risk but barely helps (1.0%), because ransomware still reaches it. Adding one offline copy removes the shared threats, and the risk falls to $1.6 \times 10^{-7}$. The exact probabilities are made up; the lesson is not: **correlated** failures, not independent ones, decide whether data survives.

Two numbers set a backup plan:

- **RPO** (recovery point objective): how much recent data you can afford to lose, which sets how often backups run. Nightly backups mean an RPO of up to 24 hours.
- **RTO** (recovery time objective): how long restoring may take. Restoring 100 TB from tape or a cold cloud tier can take days ([Storage Hierarchy](../the-storage-hierarchy/)).

<Callout type="pitfall">
A backup that has never been restored is a hope, not a backup. Backup jobs that report success can still produce unreadable archives, miss new directories, depend on a decryption key stored only on the lost system, or take far longer to restore than the RTO allows. The final "0" in 3-2-1-1-0 means regular, timed test restores.
</Callout>

<Callout type="key">
Drives fail steadily (about 1.4% a year), sectors go unreadable, and occasionally data goes silently wrong. Redundancy with checksums and scrubbing handles hardware; only independent copies, including one offline and one offsite, handle the people, software and disasters that every online copy shares.
</Callout>
